Why are there two row counts? About 1,250 dictionary entries are only continuations: extra meanings for the entry just above them (see Reading the senses). The vocab pages join these onto the word they belong to. The All fields page keeps them as separate rows, exactly as they are in the data file.

Every page has the same search and filters. A search matches the start of a word, so war finds “war” and “warfare” but not “towards”.

Additional Info

Everything else worth knowing, separated by semicolons:

  • Nouns: gender (masculine, feminine, neuter, common) and kind (person, place name, proper name, group, abstract…), plus “Greek-type” for Greek-style endings.
  • Verbs: transitive, intransitive, + dative, + ablative, impersonal, semi-deponent, compound of sum, defective, “perfect forms only (present meaning)” for verbs like odi.
  • Adjectives: comparative and superlative (e.g. comp. melior, sup. optimus), number of terminations, “genitive in -ius” for unus, totus etc.
  • Prepositions: the case taken (+ acc, + abl).
  • All words: the era if it isn't general (early Latin, late Latin, medieval…), the subject area (legal, military, poetic…), and any bracketed example phrases from the meanings.

English (as many columns as needed)

The meanings, split at every comma and semicolon that isn't inside brackets. A word with ten meanings fills ten English columns; most have fewer. Duplicates are removed.

Frequency

How common the word is in Latin literature, from very frequent down to very rare. See the frequency codes. Filter with “Common or higher” to get a sensible list for learning.

4. How stems work

Whitaker's dictionary doesn't store whole words. It stores stems, and endings are added to make every form. What each stem is for depends on the word type:

Nounnominative (sometimes the whole nominative, e.g. rex)all other cases (reg- → regis)––
Verbpresent (am- → amo)infinitive (am- → amare)perfect (amav- → amavi)supine / perfect participle (amat- → amatum)
Adjectivemasculine nominative (bon-)other forms (bon- → bona, bonum)comparative (meli- → melior)superlative (opti- → optimus)
Adverbpositive (bene)comparative (melius)superlative (optime)–
Numeralcardinal (un-)ordinal (prim-)distributive (singul-)adverb (semel)

For example, the semi-deponent audeo has stems aud, aud, aus, aus but no real perfect stem, so the vocab table shows audeo, audere, ausus sum.

7. Reading the senses

The Senses column is Whitaker's English definition, word for word. It uses a few conventions:

  • Semicolons separate different meanings; commas separate near-synonyms (love, like; fall in love with).
  • A line starting with | continues the meanings of the entry above it, for words with too many meanings for one line. The vocab pages join these on; All fields shows them as their own rows.
  • [square brackets] hold example phrases or idioms. The vocab pages move these into Additional Info.
  • => means “translates as” in a phrase, e.g. multum posse => have much influence.
  • (w/-cumque) at the start of a packon gives the suffix it needs, e.g. qu- + -cumque = quicumque.
  • ~ stands for the word itself inside a phrase.

8. Copying into Excel

  1. Open Vocab table or All fields and wait for page 1 to appear. Each page is around 7,600–7,900 rows, so it can take a few seconds.
  2. Click Copy page for Excel, then click cell A1 in Excel and paste (Ctrl+V).
  3. Click Page 2, copy again, and paste into the first empty row below. Repeat for pages 3–5. The header row is only copied with page 1, unless you tick include header row.

Or use Download CSV to get every matching row in one file that opens straight in Excel. Search and filters apply to both, so you can copy just the common words, or just the verbs.

Every page has the same number of English columns, so pasted pages always line up.

9. Limitations

  • No macrons. Whitaker's dictionary doesn't mark long vowels.
  • Spelling. Some words use j (e.g. jacio), and spellings follow Whitaker's sources, not any one textbook.
  • sum, esse is missing, because WORDS builds it into the program instead of the dictionary file. Its compounds (possum, absum…) are included.
  • Dictionary forms are rebuilt by rule from stems and declension/conjugation numbers. Common words have been checked, but rare Greek and archaic words may come out slightly wrong. The stems on the All fields page are always exactly what's in the data.
  • Meanings of codes come from the WORDS documentation. The data file itself only has the letters.
  • Duplicate-looking rows are usually real: a word may have a normal entry and an archaic-forms entry (e.g. amo, amare, amavi, amatum and amo, amare, amasso), or two different meanings.
